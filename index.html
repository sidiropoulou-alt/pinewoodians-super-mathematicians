Level 1 · Warm up 6 questions

Work out the value of n. Type your answer, then press Check or the Enter key.

1
3 + 4 × 2 = n
Hint: Multiply before you add. Start with 4 × 2.
Answer: n = 11
4 × 2 = 8, then 3 + 8 = 11
2
20 − 3 × 4 = n
Hint: Multiply before you subtract. Start with 3 × 4.
Answer: n = 8
3 × 4 = 12, then 20 − 12 = 8
3
2 × (3 + 5) = n
Hint: Brackets first! Work out 3 + 5.
Answer: n = 16
(3 + 5) = 8, then 2 × 8 = 16
4
12 ÷ 3 + 5 = n
Hint: Divide before you add. Start with 12 ÷ 3.
Answer: n = 9
12 ÷ 3 = 4, then 4 + 5 = 9
5
n + 2 × 6 = 20
Hint: Work out 2 × 6 first, then ask: what plus that makes 20?
Answer: n = 8
2 × 6 = 12, so n + 12 = 20, and n = 8
6
3 × n + 3 = 21
Hint: Take off the + 3 first, then ask: 3 × what gives what is left?
Answer: n = 6
21 − 3 = 18, and 3 × 6 = 18, so n = 6

⭐ Level 2 · Super Duper Challenge 7 questions

Only for super mathematicians — more steps to plan, and the mystery number can hide in the middle.

7
Super duper challenge
3 × (4 + 2) − 5 = n
Hint: Three jobs in order: brackets, multiply, subtract.
Answer: n = 13
4 + 2 = 6, 3 × 6 = 18, then 18 − 5 = 13
8
Super duper challenge
2 × n + 3 × 4 = 26
Hint: Two multiply-first jobs. Do 3 × 4, then see what is left for 2 × n.
Answer: n = 7
3 × 4 = 12, so 2 × n = 26 − 12 = 14, and n = 7
9
Super duper challenge
40 ÷ (2 + 3) + 4 × 2 = n
Hint: Brackets first, then divide and multiply, then add last.
Answer: n = 16
2 + 3 = 5, 40 ÷ 5 = 8, 4 × 2 = 8, then 8 + 8 = 16
10
Super duper challenge
(12 ÷ 3) × (2 + 3) − 5 = n
Hint: Both brackets first, then multiply, then subtract.
Answer: n = 15
12 ÷ 3 = 4 and 2 + 3 = 5, 4 × 5 = 20, then 20 − 5 = 15
11
Super duper challenge
4 × (10 − 2 × 3) = n
Hint: Inside the brackets, multiply before you subtract.
Answer: n = 16
2 × 3 = 6, 10 − 6 = 4, then 4 × 4 = 16

The last two · think before you answer

There is a trick in each of these two. Try some whole numbers first. If you are sure no whole number works, type the word impossible and press Check.

12
Read carefully
2 × n + 3 = 12
Hint: Try n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Does any of them give exactly 12?
Answer: Impossible — n would be 4½, and 4½ is not a whole number.
2 × 4 + 3 = 11 and 2 × 5 + 3 = 13, so you jump right over 12. No whole number fits.
13
Read carefully
n × 0 = 5
Hint: What do you always get when you multiply by 0?
Answer: Impossible — anything times 0 is 0, so it can never be 5.
0 × 0 = 0, 4 × 0 = 0, 100 × 0 = 0. It is always 0, so nothing can make 5.
Teacher notes & answer key (click to open)

How to run it in 10 minutes

  • 1 min — Read the four rules at the top together. Ask: “Which part do we do first?”
  • 4 min — Level 1, questions 1–6 on paper, then share strategies with a partner.
  • 3 min — Level 2 challenges 7–11, in pairs. Question 8 has two multiply-first jobs.
  • 2 min — The last two, as a class. Let them argue before you say anything.

What each level practises

  • Level 1 (1–6) — multiply or divide before adding or subtracting (1, 2, 4); brackets first (3); and two where the mystery number sits inside the sum (5, 6).
  • Level 2 (7–11) — three or four steps, two sets of brackets, brackets that must beat everything else (9, 11), and two multiply-first jobs (8).
  • The last two (12, 13) — checking whether an answer even exists. The maths is small; the thinking is big.

The two impossible problems — slow down here

  • 12 · 2 × n + 3 = 12. Put in whole numbers: 2 × 4 + 3 = 11, then 2 × 5 + 3 = 13. The answers step by 2, so they jump straight over 12. The only solution is 4½, which is not a whole number. Ask: “Which two numbers did we jump between?”
  • 13 · n × 0 = 5. Anything times 0 is 0. If a child says n = 5, have them test it: 5 × 0 = 0, not 5. The same happens for every number they try, which is the proof.
  • Why it is worth the time — it teaches children to check by substituting, and that sometimes the puzzle is the problem, not the child. Both cards accept the word impossible as a correct answer.

Answers you may hear (and what to say)

  • “3 + 4 = 7, 7 × 2 = 14” for question 1 — they worked left to right. Ask them to find the × sign and do that part first.
  • “n = 12” for question 5 — they found 2 × 6 but forgot the rest of the equation. Point back to it and ask what else has to happen.
  • “n = 4” for question 12 — have them check it: 2 × 4 + 3 = 11, not 12.
  • “n = 5” for question 13 — have them check it: 5 × 0 = 0, not 5.
  • “This one is broken!” for 12 or 13 — agree with them, then name it: mathematicians prove when something cannot be done.
